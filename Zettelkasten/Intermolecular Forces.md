# Intermolecular Forces

## Notes

Intermolecular forces are the attractive forces between molecules and are weaker than covalent or ionic bonds. There are different types of intermolecular forces and each one has a different weight on contribution to their role in a substances properties.

London dispersion forces are found in all substances and are the result of temporary electron cloud distortions from induced dipole moments which is caused from the motion of the electrons themselves. The larger the electron cloud i.e. the bigger the molecule then the stronger the London dispersion forces. This is due to polarizability which is the ease in which electron clouds can be distorted. Also molecular shape influence dispersion forces as an isomer with more surface area will have stronger forces since there is more contact surface.

Dipole-dipole forces exist in polar molecules and are due to permanent dipoles from the net bond polarity. London dispersion forces are stronger than dipole-dipole forces even in non polar molecules if they are the same size.

Hydrogen bonding is a force between molecules that contain a hydrogen bonded to a very small very electronegative element and has only been observed in bonds to nitrogen, oxygen and fluorine H-N H-O H-F. These are significantly stronger than typical dipole-dipole forces and most of the time stronger than London dispersion forces. 

Ion dipole forces are between dissolved ions and the solvent they are dissolved in within a mixture. Ionic bonds are the strongest forces between discrete compounds and are significantly stronger than intermolecular forces.

Phase changes occur due to energy overcoming intermolecular forces or the intermolecular forces are stronger than the energy in the system.

Vapor pressure is the amount of pressure of an evaporated gas at equilibrium and evaporation and condensation are occurring at the same rate at a constant pressure in a closed container. Normal boiling point is the temperature that a substance will begin to evaporate when at 1 atm. Vapor pressure is inversely proportional to the boiling point of a substance.

During a phase change the phase that is being transitioned from will remain at the same temperature while the phase being transitioned to will be either higher or lower depending on the type of phase change. i.e. boiling water will remain at 100°C while the water vapor will be >100°C. The potential energy of intermolecular forces is what keeps molecules together while kinetic energy tries to pull them apart. This interplay of energy is what influences phase changes. Temperature change influences kinetic energy which in turn affects intermolecular forces. Pressure can also affect phase changes but the most dramatic changes are observed in gases.

Phase changes are measured in the change of enthalpy ($\Delta H$ - $\frac{kJ}{mol}$) at 1 atm and the current temperature.
- Gas -> Liquid -> Solid the particles increase in attraction and lose energy which is released into the surroundings as heat and is exothermic
- Solid -> Liquid -> Gas the energy absorbed by the particles overcome their attractions to each other and is endothermic
- Solid -> Liquid called $\Delta H$ of fusion or melting
- Liquid -> Solid called $\Delta H$ of freezing has same value but opposite sign of fusion
- Liquid -> Gas called $\Delta H$ of vaporization 
- Gas -> Liquid called $\Delta H$ of combustion has same value but opposite sign of vaporization
- Solid -> Gas called $\Delta H$ of sublimation and is calculated from the combination of $\Delta H$ of melting and vaporization
- Gas -> Solid called $\Delta H$ of deposition and is the same value but opposite sign of sublimation and can also be calculated from the combination of $\Delta H$ of condensation and freezing

It typically take less energy to melt a solid than it does to vaporize a liquid.

Van der Waal's distance is the measure of distance between two molecules.

At higher altitudes where there is less external pressure it takes less kinetic energy to form vapor bubbles so boiling point tends to decrease. The linearized equation of vapor pressure versus temperature is called the Clausius-Clapeyron equation $ln(P) = -\frac{\Delta H_{vap}}{R}(\frac{1}{T}) + C$ where C is a constant. This can be used to relate between different pressures and temperatures $ln(\frac{P_2}{P_1}) = -\frac{\Delta H_{vap}}{R}(\frac{1}{T_1} - \frac{1}{T_2})$.

- Regions represent the pressure and temperature where the phase is stable
- Lines represent transitions between the phases and where they are at equilibrium
- The triple point is the pressure and temperature where all three phases are in equilibrium and some substance have more than one if they have several solid and liquid forms
- Critical point is where pressure has reached critical pressure and temperature critical temperature which is when the liquid and vapor density are equal and the kinetic energy is so high that the vapor cannot be condensed

When a substance is at or past the critical point it is called a super critical fluid and behaves like a liquid and a gas.

Liquid viscosity is a measure of resistance to flow or resistance to change in arrangement and is variable depending on the size and shape of the molecules, the intermolecular forces and the temperature.

Surface tension is the measure of the amount of energy required to increase the surface area of a liquid. Molecules in a liquid want to have a minimum surface area due to the resistance to unbounding from the intermolecular attractions which is most prevalent at the surface and creates a spherical shape in nature and also increases with stronger intermolecular forces.

Capillary is the attraction or adhesive forces that can let an aqueous solution overcome gravity up a surface. Capillary action is the process of an aqueous solution rising on a surface like in a glass tube which is very polar and attracts molecules with strong intermolecular forces.

Water's unique properties arise from having O-H bonds and a bent shape which allows four hydrogen bonds for each molecules. This tetrahedral structure forms hexagonal open structure solids which is why the density of ice is less that the density of water where most substances have a higher density when solid due to the compactness of the particles. Water is also a great solvent because it separates ionic compounds through ion-dipole forces, polar non-ionic compounds from the strong hydrogen bonds, and even non-polar atmospheric gases to a limited extent. These strong bonds also lead to water having the highest specific heat capacity compared to any common liquid, a high heat of vaporization, a very high surface tension and capillarity.
