# Mixtures

## Notes

Mixtures are 2 or more different substances that are combined and can be separated through a physical process.

Heterogeneous mixtures have a varied composition throughout and has visible boundaries between substances yet some can only be seen through a microscope like milk.

Homogeneous mixtures have uniform composition throughout as the substances are blended together with no visible boundaries. These mixtures are clear and the different substances cannot be distinguished i.e. salt water.

### Separating Mixtures

The different techniques for separating mixtures are
1. Filtration - often used to separate solids from liquids and involves passing a mixture through a screen or a film with very small holes which will have the substance with smaller particles pass through and the large particulates will remain. Vacuum filtration involves reducing the pressure underneath the filter so that the smaller particulates will flow through at a higher speed.
2. Crystallization - this involves leveraging the solubility of a substance (the amount of a substance that dissolves in a fixed volume of solvent at a given temperature) by applying heat to the mixture as solubility increases with temperature. When the mixture is cooled back down the pure substance solidifies (crystallizes) and the impurities stay dissolved in whatever is left of the solvent.
3. Distillation - Separation through differences in volatility (the tendency of a substance to become a gas). There is simple distillation where the different components of the mixture have a large difference in volatility and the highly volatile substance turns into vapor and is collected in another receptacle. Fractional distillation involves multiple vaporization-condensation cycles and is used for mixtures where the components have small differences in volatility.
4. Chromatography - is another process that utilizes the solubility of substances by dissolving a mixture in a gas or liquid (this solution is called the mobile phase) and flowing it through a porous solid or viscous liquid (called the stationary phase). The different components of the mobile phase will travel through the stationary phase at varying speeds depending on their solubility where low solubility travels faster and high travels slower. A special type of chromatography is called Gas-Liquid Chromatography (GLC) where the mobile phase in an inert gas that is packed with the stationary phase and emerges out of the gases container through a detector. 

### Solubility

Homogenous mixtures are also known as solutions and typically involve a solvent (which is normally water) and a solute which is the substance that dissolved in the solvent. These solutions can be expressed mathematically in terms of their ratio of moles of solute substance to the entire solutions volume which is called the molarity (M) which is derived from moles per liter (mol/L). Percent by mass is the mass of the solute compared to the entire solution or mass of solute/mass of solution which is the mass ratio and then multiplying by one hundred.

Dilution of a solution involves adding more solvent to the solution while maintaining the same amount of moles of the solute. The volume will change which changes the molarity since the moles of solute will remain the same. When calculating the amount of volume to achieve a certain molarity or vice versa the formula M<sub>1</sub>V<sub>1</sub> = M<sub>2</sub>V<sub>2</sub> can be used deriving from the original concentration. Since molarity = moles / volume one unit can be calculated if the other 2 are known.
