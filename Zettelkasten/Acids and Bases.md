# Acids and Bases

## Notes

Acids and bases are classified in different way by three separate theories. The original is Arrehenius's which states that an acid is a substance that has and loses a proton (H<sup>+</sup>) when dissolved in water and bases have and lose a hydroxide ion (OH<sup>-</sup>) when dissolved in water. Later Bronsted and Lowry proposed that while the acid definition remained the same the definition for bases expanded to include any substance that has a lone pair of electrons to accept a proton (H<sup>+</sup>). Later Lewis proposed a similar theory but classified acids as a substance with a vacant orbital to accept a lone electron pair to form a new bond.

Acids are molecular compounds and do not present acidic characteristics unless dissolved in a solvent. Acids act like ionic compounds when in water and have a monatomic or polyatomic anion.
Oxoacids have consist of hydrogen and a polyatomic anion that contains oxygen while binary acids consist of hydrogen paired with one other element. In an acid not all hydrogens are acidic and are only classified as such under certain conditions like a high difference in electronegativity from its bonded element.

Binary acids naming conventions start with hydro- and then the anion ending in -ic.
Oxoacids are named from the polyatomic anion and derived from the ionic name of the anion i.e. -ate becomes -ic and -ite become -ous.

Acids produce H<sup>+</sup> ions (protons) or H<sub>3</sub>O<sup>+</sup> (hydronium) when dissolved in water and strong acids will ionize completely while weak acids will dissociate very little. In oxoacids the H is bonded to the oxygen. Hydronium is essentially a lone proton surrounded by water molecules but because of the unstable nature of a lone proton it cannot remain completely as a lone entity and heavily attracts polar molecules like water but does not bond to them.

Bases produce OH<sup>-</sup> ions when dissolved in water and strong bases will have either OH<sup>-</sup> or O<sup>2-</sup> as part of its structure and dissociate completely into ions in water. Weak bases do not contain OH<sup>-</sup> ions but an electron pair on N which attracts H atoms in water to produce OH<sup>-</sup> and dissociates very little in water.

Conjugate acid-base pairs are the reflective acid and base over a reaction for example when an acid is a reactant then there is a product that is its conjugate base. Conjugate pairs are inverse in strength, a weak acid will have a strong conjugate base and vice versa. This is due to acid base reactions favoring the side of the reaction with a weaker acid/base since they are in a more stable state and not readily able to either accept (base) or donate (acid) a proton.

The change in enthalpy of an acid-base neutralization reaction between a strong acid and a strong base is always the same value because they both completely dissociate into their respective ions and have a net ionic equation of $H_3O^+(aq) + OH^-(aq) \rightarrow H_2O(l)$ since the other ions in the acid and base are just spectator ions.

Water ($H_2O$) goes through a process called self-ionization or autoionization which is a process where water molecules dissociate slightly on their own to produce hydronium and hydroxide ions $H_2O(l) + H_2O(l) \rightarrow H_3O^+(aq) + OH^-(aq)$. This process helps quantify the strengths or acids and bases by using its equilibrium constant or ion product constant denoted for this specific reaction as $K_w$ where $K_w = [H_3O^+][OH^-] = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-14}$ at 25°C. $K_w$ leaves out $H_2O$ since it is a liquid and its concentration is always constant as per the rules of equilibrium equations. Hydronium and hydroxide take equal parts in autoionization of water so they are at equal value $[H_3O^+] = 1.0 \cdot 10^{-7} = [OH^-]$ and because of equilibrium when either one decreases in concentration the other will increase in concentration proportionally. The comparison of these concentrations determines if the solution is Acidic: $[H_3O^+] \gt [OH^-]$ Basic: $[H_3O^+] \lt [OH^-]$ or Neutral: $[H_3O^+] = [OH^-]$. This also gives the means to find one or the other's concentration $[H_3O^+] = \frac{K_w}{[OH^-]}$ or $[OH^-] = \frac{K_w}{[H_3O^+]}$ when $K_w$ is known at a certain temperature.

A p-scale is the mathematical process of taking quantities that span over multiple magnitudes and are very small and converting them into more manageable linear positive quantities by taking the negated common logarithm of those values $n = values$ then $-log(n)$. The standard for measuring the acidity of a solution is called the pH scale (p for p-scale and H for hydrogen ions) which is measured from 1-14 and is calculated by $pH = -log([H_3O^+])$ where the lower the number the more acidic the solution and the lower the number the more basic the solution. There is also the pOH scale (like pH but for OH ions) also range 1-14 and is calculated $pH = -log([OH^-])$ which is opposite of pH where lower is more basic and higher is more acidic. The relationship of these scales is due to the $K_w$ constant and is related by $-log(K_w) = -log([H_3O^+][OH^-])$ which gives the equation $pH + pOH = 14$ at 25°C. When given a pH or pOH value and want to find the respective concentration of hydronium or hydroxide atoms $[H_3O^+] = 10^{-pH}$ or $[OH^-] = 10^{-pOH}$.

pH is typically measure with test strips which use organic molecules that change color at different pH levels. There are also more accurate and advanced pH meters which use electrodes and measure the difference in charge caused by a difference in positive hydronium ions versus negative hydroxide ions.

An acid ionization constant denoted $K_a$ is an equilibrium constant for acids that can be used to quantify the relative strengths between other acids. This equilibrium is measure from the amount of hydronium that dissociates from the acid where the more that dissociate the stronger the acid hence a large $K_a$ value the stronger the acid. A basic ionization constant $K_b$ is the same thing but for bases.

Polyprotic acids are acids that have more than one acidic hydrogen. Polyprotic acids have more than one $K_a$ value for each hydrogen that can be donated where the subsequent $K_a$ values drop drastically for each level $K_{a1} >> K_{a2} >> K_{a3}$ etc.

Strong acids come in two forms hydrohalic (HCl, HBr, HI nut not HF) and oxoacids where the number of oxygen atoms exceed the number of ionizable (acidic) hydrogens by a factor of two or more. Strong bases are water soluble compounds containing either $O^{2-}$ or $OH^-$ ions with the cation in the compound being one the most active metals (group one and two of periodic table). Since strong acids dissociate completely $[H_3O^+] \approx [Acid]$ their reaction formula can be written as a complete reaction not a equilibrium reaction $HA + H_2O \rightarrow H_3O^+ + A^-$. This also goes for strong bases $[OH^-] \approx [Base]$ hence $MOH + H_2O \rightarrow M^+ + OH^-$. Both strong acids and strong bases are strong electrolytes because they dissociate completely into ions. This complete dissociation also makes calculating the pH of a solution pretty straightforward since hydronium concentration basically equals the acid concentration their values can be interchanged and the concentration of the acid multiplied by the mole ratio to the hydronium $pH = -log([H_3O^+]) = -log(n_{acid} \cdot [Acid])$.

When calculating the pH of a weak acid or base either one of the $K_a$ $K_b$ $[H_3O^+]$ or $[OH^-]$ must be known and can derive the other values through the same methods of any equilibrium equation. When there is a K value not associated with the substance like a $K_a$ for a base then the other can be found by taking the K value of the conjugate pair and dividing the water autoionization equilibrium constant $K_w$. For example to get the $K_b$ of $F^-$ since $K_a$ is known for $HF$ then we can find $K_b(F^-) = \frac{K_w}{K_a(HF)}$ and determine the $[OH^-]$ from there. The percent of dissociation ($\frac{[Acid]dis}{[Acid]init} \times 100$) of weak acids tends to increase the lower the initial concentration of that acid is due to there being "more room" in the solution and slightly favoring more moles of substances to make up for the space.

Most main group metals transfer electrons to oxygen making metal oxides ionic which act as a strong base in water producing hydroxide (OH<sup>-</sup>) ions from O<sup>2-</sup> and react with acids. Non-metals share electrons with oxygen to make covalent oxides that react with water to make acids producing protons or hydronium ions (H<sup>+</sup>/H<sub>3</sub>O<sup>+</sup>) and react with bases. Some metals and many metalloids form amphoteric oxides and can act as a base or an acid in water. Elements that tend to form basic oxides trend towards the bottom left of the periodic table while elements that tend to form acidic oxides trend towards the top right. For binary acids localized to group 16 and 17 the acidity increases across a period due to difference in electronegativity and down a group due to bond length and strength (how easily a hydrogen can be separated from the compound) while for oxoacids the strength also increases across a period due to electronegativity but the number of oxygen atoms and the oxidation number of the central atom have a greater influence on the strength of the acid.

Cations of strong bases do not affect the pH of a solution as they are too small and have minimal charge. Cations that are a conjugate weak acid of a weak base react with water to create hydronium ions which lowers the pH of a solution. Highly charged hydrated metal ions have a high charge density that can pull a hydrogen from a water molecules O-H bond thus behaving like an acid and lowers the pH of a solution.

Anions of a strong acid do not affect the pH of a solution which is proven by the fact strong acids dissociate completely and the anion does not have an affinity to accept a proton making them spectator ions. Anions of weak acids are the conjugate base which attracts protons to produce hydroxide ions increasing the pH of a solution.

Amphiprotic anions that are from polyprotic acids with an ionizable proton still attached can effect the pH in either direction. This can be determined by comparing its $K_a$ and $K_b$ values which will most likely have to be derived from the polyprotic acid's separate $K_a$ values for each proton ionized. Whichever K value is higher is the effect the anion will have on the pH ($K_a > K_b = pH -$ and $K_a < K_b = pH+$).

When a salt dissolved in water the pH value of the solution depends on the strength of the basic cation and the acidic anion and how they affect pH individually and calculating which side has a larger affect on the pH which also goes for amphiprotic anions and their basic or acidic affinities. When both the cation and anion are neutral the solution is neutral.

Water exerts what is called a leveling effect on strong acids and bases causing them to all appear equally strong since they dissociate completely. To measure the relevant strength of strong acids a weaker base must be used as a solvent (like a weak acid) and then the relevant strengths become more apparent between the strong acids.
