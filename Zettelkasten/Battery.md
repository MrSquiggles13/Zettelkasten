# Battery

## Notes

A battery is a series of self-contained voltaic cells. Self contained batteries come in two main forms primary and secondary.

Primary batteries are expendable and cannot be recharged. They typically have their initial reactants as far from equilibrium as is feasible and when that equilibrium is reached the battery can no longer do work and is considered "dead". 

Alkaline batteries are the most common primary battery and have MnO<sub>2</sub> plus graphite reduced at the cathode and powdered zinc in a gel oxidized at the anode. The electrolyte used is KOH in gel form mixed with water. 

Another primary battery that is fairly common are silver button batteries which have a Zn anode with a zinc and KOH gel that is separated by a plastic membrane from the steel cased cathode that has Ag<sub>2</sub>O pellets in graphite.

Secondary batteries are similar to primary but are rechargeable. Electrical energy can be applied to a secondary battery to initiate the nonspontaneous reversed cell reaction and form more reactant.

An example is a lead acid battery that are typically found in cars. These have six cells in series that are 2.1 V each or about 12 V total. The anode consists of a spongy lead with high surface area while the cathode is a lead oxide (PbO<sub>2</sub>) also with a high surface area. The cells are immersed in a 45 M solution of H<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub> electrolyte and are separated by fiberglass protective sheets to prevent short circuits. Both the anode and the cathode form Pb<sup>2+</sup> ions which form PbSO<sub>4</sub> reacting with the electrolyte. When recharging the PbSO<sub>4</sub> reacts with water to reform PbO<sub>2</sub>, Pb and H<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub> which can be used again in the forward spontaneous reaction. 

Another fairly common secondary battery is the nickel-metal hydroxide battery that replaced older nickel-cadmium batteries due to cadmium being fairly radioactive. These batteries have an anode made of an alloy composed of transition and inner transition metals which can readily absorb H<sup>+</sup> ions from electrolytes when charging forming a metal hydroxide. The cathode normally consists nickel being reduced to NiO(OH) and both are immersed in a basic electrolyte like KOH.

By far the most common secondary battery is the lithium-ion battery which are found in all types of modern devices such as phones laptops flashlights etc. The anode consists of lithium that is between sheets of graphite essentially forming LiC<sub>6</sub> while the cathode is some form of transition metal oxide most commonly CoO<sub>2</sub>. Fairly common electrolyte is a 1 M solution of LiPF<sub>6</sub> which has to be dissolved in a nonaqueous organic solvent since lithium is highly reactive which can be ethylene carbonate, dimethyl carbonate, or diethyl carbonate among others. Lithium ions are conducted by the electrolyte and flow from the anode to the cathode to combine with the metal oxide such as CoO<sub>2</sub> to form LiCoO<sub>2</sub>. When a charge is applied to the system the reaction reverses to produce more reactants.

Solid state batteries are newer very uncommon secondary batteries that use a solid electrolyte made of a ceramic or polymer and has an anode made of pure lithium metal.

A fuel cell or flow battery is not self-contained and have reactants that are normally a type of fuel with O<sub>2</sub> that enter the cell which reacts with an electrolyte barrier producing products that leave the cell and generate electricity then recombine through controlled combustion reactions with water and heat as a byproduct.
