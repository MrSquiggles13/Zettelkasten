# Electron Configuration

## Notes

Orbitals are defined by three quantum numbers and a fourth reserved for electrons.
1. Principle quantum number ($n$) determines the shell or distance from the nucleus and energy level that is an integer value starting from 1. 
2. Angular momentum (Azimuthal) quantum number ($l$) is the shape of the orbital and is also an integer number with a range from $(0, n - 1)$.
3. Magnetic quantum number ($m_l$) is the direction and orientation of the orbital and is a number in the range $(-l, l)$.
4. Electron spin quantum number ($m_s$) is the intrinsic magnetic field an electron generates based on the orientation of is poles and is either $\frac{1}{2}$ or $-\frac{1}{2}$.

The value of the angular momentum quantum number classifies the shape of the orbital 
- $l = 1$ denoted by $s$ and is a sphere and only has one form
- $l = 2$ denoted by $p$ and are 2 lobes and has 3 forms $p_z$ $p_x$ $p_y$
- $l = 2$ denoted by $d$ and are 4 lobes and has 5 forms $d_{xy}$ $d_{yz}$ $d_{xz}$ $d_{z^{2}}$ $d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}$ 
- $l = 3$ denoted by $f$ and is a complex shape with many forms

Orbitals have discrete energy levels which is called quantized energy and the levels called quanta. For each principle energy level denoted by the principle quantum number ($n$) there are certain configurations of sub levels
- $n = 1$ sublevels 1s
- $n = 2$ sublevels 2s and 2p
- $n = 3$ sublevels 3s, 3p, and 3d
- $n = 4$ sublevels 4s, 4p, 4d and 4f
- $n = 5+$ sublevels ns, np, nd and nf

Orbital rules determine the electron arrangement of an element in its ground state (lowest energy state)
- Aufbau principle - electrons occupy the lowest energy orbitals first.
- Pauli exclusion principle - A maximum of two electrons can occupy an orbital but they must be of opposite spins.
- Hund's rule - Electrons are distributed into orbitals of identical energy level in a way that has the max amount of unpaired electrons with parallel spin. 

Electron configuration notation involves subshells labelled by their principle number (n or shell) their orbital shape (l value) and the amount of electrons in that subshell. example Li = $1s^22s^1$. There is also a symbolic notation that involved using arrows $\uparrow \downarrow$ for spin inside of boxes that represent each sub orbital in an orbital shell. An abbreviated version involves the last periods noble gas in brackets with the difference in electron configuration notated after $Li = [He]2s^1$.

Group 1 & 2 (alkali and alkaline earth metals) have an $s$ orbital as their highest energy level shell, group 13-18 have a $p$ orbital as their highest energy level shell, transition metals have a $d$ orbital as their highest energy level shell and inner transition metals have an $f$ orbital as their highest energy level shell.

For transition metals $d$ orbitals will fill before the (n+1) $p$ orbital and after the (n+1) $s$ orbital.
Inner transition metals (Period 6 and 7) have their shells filled in this order: ns, first one of (n-1)d, entire (n-2)f, remaining nine of (n-1)d, then np.

Valence electrons are electrons that are the easiest to remove from an atom. For main group elements their valence electrons are in their highest level shell (n) and have the amount equal to their roman numeral group number (IIIA) = 3 valence electrons. When main group elements become ions the cations will lose valence electrons to equal the amount of electrons the period above's noble gas has and anions will gain electrons to equal electrons of the same period noble gas. When metals lost their valence electrons their p shell electrons are first followed by s shell then finally d shell.

Outer electrons are the sum of electrons in the n shell while inner electrons are total electrons minus valence electrons.

Electrostatic effects make multi electron atoms different from hydrogen. A higher nuclear charge (Z) or more protons in the nucleus, increases nucleus-electron attraction and lowers the sublevel energy i.e. stabilizes the atom. Electrons nuclear attraction is counteracted by the other electrons repulsion due to like charges and this leads to shielding or reducing the nuclear charge to an effective nuclear charge (Z<sub>eff</sub>). Nuclear penetration is what give s orbitals a lower energy state since their probability field places their electrons closer to the nucleus at certain instances than p orbitals of same shell even though p orbitals have a closer probability field to the nucleus on average. This causes the electrons to penetrate the inner shell and create a lower energy level by decreasing shielding.

Effective nuclear charge ($Z_{eff}$) of an atom is calculated by $Z_{eff} = Z - S$ where $Z$ is the number of protons in the nucleus and S is the screening constant or number of core electrons. Core electrons are the electrons that make up the inner shell, do not contribute to chemical reactions and have a strong attraction to the nucleus. $Z_{eff}$ is generally assumed to be the valence electrons with some exceptions.  Effective nuclear charge increases across a period since valence electrons are added but core electrons remain the same while the number of protons increase. $Z_{eff}$ remains basically the same down the group with negligible differences.

Ionization energy ($IE$) is the energy (in kilojoules) required to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of an atom or an ion in its gaseous form. Each subsequent level of ionization energies ($IE_1$ $IE_2$ ... $IE_x$) is the energy required after $(x - 1)$ electrons have already been removed. $IE$ drastically increases when a level reaches the core electrons since their attraction is so strong to the nucleus. $IE$ has a general trend of increasing from bottom left to top right of the periodic table due to atomic size and $Z_{eff}$. The less $IE$ that is needed the more energy that is released to the surroundings. From group 2 to 3 $IE$ drops slightly due to the p orbital having an easier to remove single electron and group 15 to 16 has a slight drop as well but due to a single electron pair in a p orbital is easier to remove as a half filled p orbital is a more stable state.

Electron Affinity is the energy change that occurs when one mole of electrons are added to one mole of an atom or ion in its gaseous state. $EA$ is sometimes positive if it takes energy to make a new energy level (shell) or already paired valence electrons. $EA$ is most of the time negative since electron attraction to the nucleus is very strong and a desired stable outcome but $EA$ has levels like $IE$ and $EA_2$ is always positive since energy must be absorbed in order to overcome the electrostatic repulsion of adding another electron to a negatively charged ion.

A low $IE$ and $\pm EA$ means an element is a strong reducing agent and has a tendency to be oxidized while a high $IE$ and $-EA$ means an element is a strong oxidizing agent and has a tendency to be reduced.

Transition metals rarely attain a noble gas formation due to their d orbital besides Sc<sup>+2</sup> and occasionally Ti<sup>4+</sup>. Transition elements typically form more than one cation by losing all of their $s$ orbital electrons and some of their $d$ orbital electrons.

Magnetism can be determined from the configuration of the electrons in their orbitals. Paramagnetic is when an atom or an ion has at least one unpaired electron and is attracted to an external magnetic field. Diamagnetic is when an atom or an ion has electrons that are all paired and is not attracted too or slightly repulsed by an external magnetic field.

Excited electrons are typically valence electrons and will move to the next highest energy level orbital i.e. 2s -> 2p or 4s -> 3d.
