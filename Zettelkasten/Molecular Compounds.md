# Molecular Compounds

## Notes

Molecular compounds  are formed between two non metals and are discrete molecular units. Molecular compounds can be found as gas, liquid or solids, when solids are very soft, have a low melting and boiling point, and a low density. Molecular compounds are poor conductors of electricity in pure form and are either weak or non electrolytes.

Molecular compound naming conventions use Greek prefixes to label the amount of element in a compound with the last element having an -ide suffix. When the prefix is more than one syllable and the element starts with a vowel the ending vowel in the prefix is dropped i.e. Carbon Monoxide (CO). Mono prefix is not used for the first element. The element of a lower group is named first and if the elements are of the same group the element in a higher period is named first. The exception is a pairing of a halogen (Group 17/VIIA) with oxygen where the halogen is always named first.

Organic compounds are typically complex molecular structures that consist of chains, rings, or branches of carbon atoms bonded to hydrogen atoms along with oxygen nitrogen and a few others. Hydrocarbons are the simplest type of organic compound and contain only carbon and hydrogen atoms. Alkanes or the simplest type of hydrocarbons and most function as fuel such as methane, propane, butane, and mixtures for gasoline. The simplest alkanes are straight-chain alkanes as their carbon chains have no branches. Alkanes are named by the root derived from the number of carbons with the suffix -ane. The first four count of carbons are different than the greek prefix (methane, ethane, propane, butane) but the rest five and up use Greek prefix (pentane, hexane, heptane, octane etc.).
