# Molecular Orbital Theory

## Notes

Molecular orbital theory is used to help describe electron behavior as wave functions in molecules and are called Molecular Orbitals (MO). Molecular orbital theory helps account for properties that are not able to be explained using [[Valence Bond Theory]] such as magnetic properties and excited states of some molecules. MO have discrete energy levels, three dimensional probability region and each orbital can only contain two electrons of opposite spin which adheres to typical [[Electron Configuration]].

MOs form by the overlap of atomic orbitals that are similar in size and energy. These orbitals are conserved which means if two atomic orbitals overlap they create two molecular orbitals. MOs can be either constructive addition like in VB theory and are called bonding orbitals which are lower in energy than atomic orbitals or destructive addition which are called antibonding orbitals where the electron densities are shifted away from each other and has higher energy levels than atomic orbitals. The area between nuclei of an antibond is called a node and is a region of zero electron density.

MO bond order is determined by the formula 1/2(#Bonding e<sup>-</sup> - $Antibonding e<sup>-</sup>) and can help determine if a bond will form in certain reactions if the bond order is greater than 0 then it is possible but if it is 0 then the bond is not possible.

When filling an MO diagram where the bonds and antibonds are laid out from lowest energy at the bottom to the highest energy at the top, electrons are paired from the lowest energy MO to the highest. For some molecules sigma bonds are higher in energy than the pi bonds but is rare.
