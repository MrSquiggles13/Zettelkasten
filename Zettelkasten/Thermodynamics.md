# Thermodynamics

## Notes

Thermodynamics is the study of energy and its transformations.

The first law of thermodynamics (law of conservation of energy) states that the total energy of the universe is constant $\Delta E_{universe} = \Delta E_{system} + \Delta E_{surroundings} = 0$.

Energy is the capacity to do work, produce heat, or cause change. Energy is conserved as it transfers between objects and is indirectly measured by the change in temperature. Energy's SI unit is the joule (J) which is equal to $1 kg \cdot m^{2} / s^{2}$ which is derived from work w = F x d where F = force and d = distance and since force accelerates a mass it has units of mass times acceleration so in units $F(kg \cdot m/s^{2}) \times m = kg \cdot m^{2}/s^{2}$. 

Lower energy states are preferrable and more stable than higher energy states.

Potential energy is defined by the position of an object relative to another.
Kinetic energy is the motion of an object.

Potential energy is converted to kinetic energy as the system reverts to a more stable state.

A system is the focus or "universe" observed while the surroundings is everything else. The surroundings are technically everything besides the system typically the surroundings that directly affect or affected by the system are considered.

Thermochemistry is a branch of thermodynamics and deals with heat in chemical and physical changes of substances.

Internal energy (E) is the sum of potential and kinetic energy. Change in internal energy ($\Delta$E) is the loss or gain of energy in the system. Changes in energy are determined if work (w) is done onto or by the system and if thermal energy (q) has left or entered the system and the formula is $\Delta E = q + w$. Based on the first law of thermodynamics the energy released by the system is absorbed by the surroundings and vice versa.

Pressure volume work is a type of mechanical work where the volume of a system changes in the presence of an external pressure or $P \cdot \Delta V$ where work (w) = -$P \cdot \Delta V$ and the units are in $atm \cdot L$ where $1 atm \cdot L = 101.3 J$

Heat (or thermal energy) is the energy transferred from an object of a higher temperature to an object with a lower temperature and when q > 0 the heat energy is absorbed by the system from the surroundings and is considered endothermic while when q < 0 the heat energy leaves the system into the surroundings and is considered exothermic. Heat is measured in joules(J) calorie(cal) and Calorie(Cal) and 1 cal = 4.184J 1 Cal = 1000 cal calorie used to be measured by the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 gram of water from 14.5°C to 15.5°C but now is defined by 1 cal = 4.184 J. There are also British thermal units (Btu) which are less common but is the energy required to raise the temperature of 1 lb of water 1°F and 1 Btu = 1055J.

Specific heat capacity is the relationship between energy, temperature, and the mass of a substance. It is the amount of heat required to increase the temperature of 1 gram of a substance by 1°C and the units are (J/g°C). Water's specific heat is 4.184 J/g°C.

To find heat (q) use the equation $q = m * C * \Delta T$ where m = mass in grams C = specific heat capacity and $\Delta T$ = change in temperature. 

Heat capacity (C) is the quantity of heat to change the temperature of an object which is measured in joules per kelvin (J/K).

Adiabatic compression is when a system has no temperature change and the change of energy is only determined by the change of work.

Enthalpy change ($\Delta$H) is the heat change under constant pressure which is the condition most reactions occur under and $\Delta E = \Delta H$ when work is zero (w = 0). Enthalpy is affected by the physical state changes that occur and the state that a substance is in. 

Enthalpy of formation $\Delta H_{F}$ is the formation of a mole of a compound from its constituent elements in their most stable form. Standard enthalpy of formation $\Delta H°_{F}$ is the enthalpy of a pure substance in standard conditions of 1 atm and 298K. The standard enthalpy of formation can be used to calculate the standard change of enthalpy of a reaction by manipulating thermochemical equations to have the same reactants and products of the original reaction. The equation for the standard enthalpy of a reaction is $\Delta H°_{rxn} = \sum n \Delta H°_{F} (products) - \sum n \Delta H°_{F} (reactants)$ 

Thermochemical equations show enthalpy change in a reaction and is proportional to the moles of each reactant and product. Thermochemical equations are reversable and can have stoichiometric fractions in their formulas as well but must be considered when calculating the enthalpy change.

Hess's law states that if a reaction is carried out in steps that the change in enthalpy will be equal to the sum of enthalpy changes of those individual steps $\sum \Delta H_{step} = \Delta H_{reaction}$ 

Calorimetry is a method to determine the heat change of a system or reaction by measuring the heat change of its surroundings. This involves having a reaction to be done in water and measuring the temperature change of the water then reversing the sign to equate the heat of the reaction. This can also measure the temperature change or the energy used to change the temperature of a substance that is hotter or colder than the water when it is placed in the water.

A spontaneous change of a system occurs under specific conditions without continuous input of energy from the environment while a nonspontaneous change occurs only from a continuous input of energy from the environment. Under given conditions if a reaction is spontaneous in one direction then the reaction is nonspontaneous in the opposite direction. A reaction being spontaneous has no indicator on the rate of the reaction just that given ample time the process will happen on its own after some initial input. This initial input is called the activation energy and the reaction will continue on its own without additional input from the environment. 

The first law of thermodynamics and the change in enthalpy cannot predict the direction of a spontaneous change. Previously it was thought that an exothermic reaction is spontaneous while an endothermic reaction is nonspontaneous but it has been proven that is not the case. Spontaneous change relates to the freedom of motion and dispersal of energy through the quantization of energy like electronic, translational, relational and vibrational states. The different variations of these states and their permutations are called microstates which have the same total energy of a system and over time each microstate is just as likely as all the others. The number of ways this energy can be dispersed for a system is the number of microstates a system has. 

Entropy is a thermodynamic quantity that relates to the number of ways the energy of a system can be dispersed through the motions of its particles. Entropy is denoted $S$ and is related to the number of microstates by the equation $S = kln(W)$ where $W = microstates$ and $k = Boltzmann's \ constant$ which is the ideal gas constant divided by Avogadro's number $k = \frac{R}{N_A} = 1.38 \cdot 10^{-23} \frac{J}{K}$. When there are more microstates entropy increases and vice versa. The change in entropy is $\Delta S = S_{final} - S_{initial}$ since entropy is a state function. A positive change in entropy shifts to more microstates for the system and a negative change in entropy less microstates.

Quantifying the change in entropy can be done from the number of microstates like doubling the volume of a container filled with a pure noble gas and measuring the amount of states in each area for each atom and raising that state amount to the power of the amount of atoms i.e. 1 mol of Xenon would be $\Delta S = S_f - S_i = kln(W_f) - kln(W_i) = kln(\frac{W_f}{W_i}) = kln(2^{N_A}) = kN_aln(2) = 5.76 \frac{J}{K}$. Also changes in heat such as a gas being heated and doing work on its surroundings which uses the formula $\Delta S = \frac{q_{rev}}{T}$ where $T$ is the temperature where the heat change occurs and $q_{rev}$ is the heat absorbed with the subscript rev refers to the reversible process that occurs in very small increments such that the system remains in equilibrium and the directions can reverse by infinitesimal reversal of conditions. This allows the increase and decrease in microstates to be unaffected by any outside variables and can be incremented in the same steps as can be decremented. This can be seen using a closed container filled with a noble gas, a heat reservoir underneath so that heat is not lost to the environment and a piston at the top of the container with a beaker of sand weighing it down to make the container volume 1 liter. When sand is either added or removed the system's energy is preserved and only affected by the volume increase or decrease that is extremely small and since energy is preserved the system will gain/lose heat in the same amount that work is done to/from the beaker of sand on the piston. If grains are taken from the beaker one at a time until the container increases from 1 L to 2 L and integrate all those changes in heat to get $q_{rev}$ then use the previous formula to find the change in entropy $\Delta S = \frac{q_{rev}}{T} = \frac{1718 J}{298 K} = 5.76 \frac{J}{K}$.

The second law of thermodynamics states that all real processes occur spontaneously in the direction that increases the entropy of the universe $\Delta S_{universe} = \Delta S_{system} + \Delta S_{surroundings} > 0$.

The third law of thermodynamics states that a perfect crystal has zero entropy at absolute zero due to a perfect crystal having all its particles aligned flawlessly and having minimum energy at absolute zero it has only one microstate $S = klnW = kln(1) = 0$.

Entropy is a state function but also has a zero point that is used to determine the absolute values of entropy. The standard molar entropy ($S°$) measured in $\frac{J}{mol \cdot K}$ is the measurement of a substance in standard state (1 atm for gases, 1 M for solutions, stable form for pure substances solid or liquid) at 298 K and is derived from the summation of the incremental change in entropy from absolute zero to 298 K using very small increments (integral).

Entropy increases as more heat is absorbed or temperature increases since the average kinetic energy of the particles increases. Entropy also increases for endothermic phase changes solid -> liquid -> gas due to more freedom of motion of the particles and has a larger increase from liquid -> gas than solid -> liquid. Typically entropy increases when a solid or liquid solute dissolves in a solvent $S_{solution} > (S_{solute} + S_{solvent})$. For ionic solutes the amount of dissolution (more increases entropy) the restriction of movement from the polarized water molecules (more attraction decreases entropy) and the charge of the ions affecting intermolecular forces (stronger decreases entropy) can affect the entropy of the entire system. For molecular solute the increase in entropy is typically very small when dissolved in water due to the molecules not separating and the formation of the hydrogen bonds with the water molecules. Entropy decreases when a gas dissolves in a liquid or solid since the gases particle movement is heavily reduced but a gas dissolved with a gas increases entropy since the separation and mixing of gases causes more possible microstates. Entropy increases for heavier atoms or for heavier compounds of similar composition since their energy levels become closer together with size giving more possible pathways for electrons to move. Entropy is higher in allotropes that allow for greater degrees of freedom like cyclopentene (circular fixed) < pentene (chain like rotations one single bond) which entropy also increases with chain length which pentene is also greater. Entropy increases with the complexity of a of a compound or how many atoms it has, this is due to more possible atomic motions between bonds. Entropy is most affected by physical state and in a reaction the moles of gas than all the other factors.

Standard entropy of reaction $\Delta S°_{rxn}$ is the entropy change that occurs when all the reactants and products are in their standard states. $\Delta S°_{rxn}$ is positive when the moles of gas increase and negative when it decreases. $\Delta S°_{rxn}$ can be calculated from the reactants and products $S°$ values $\Delta S°_{rxn} = \sum mS°_{products} - \sum nS°_{reactants}$ when $m = moles \ products$ and $n = moles \ reactants$.

Surroundings in essence have no change in temperature and either add or remove heat from a system. When a system releases heat (exothermic) the surroundings absorb it and increase in entropy while when a system absorbs heat (endothermic) the surroundings release it and decrease in entropy. The effect on the change in entropy of the surroundings is amplified the lower the surrounding's initial temperature. $\Delta S_{surr}$ is directly related to the opposite change in the heat of the system and inversely related to the temperature at which the heat was transferred $\Delta S_{surr} = - \frac{q_{sys}}{T}$ where at constant pressure $\Delta S_{surr} = - \frac{\Delta H_{sys}}{T}$. If a spontaneous reaction has a negative $\Delta S_{sys}$ then the $\Delta S_{surr}$ must be positive enough for the $\Delta S_{univ}$ to be positive or zero and vice vera due to the second law of thermodynamics. A process approaching equilibrium will have a $\Delta S_{univ} > 0$ and when equilibrium is reached $\Delta S_{univ} = 0$.

Gibbs free energy is a thermodynamic quantity that is the difference between a system's enthalpy and the product of absolute temperature and system's entropy $G = H -TS$. $\Delta G$ is a measure of the spontaneity of a process and the amount of useful energy that is available. Since $\Delta S_{univ} = \Delta S_{sys} + \Delta S_{surr}$ and $\Delta S_{surr} = - \frac{\Delta H_{sys}}{T}$ then $\Delta S_{univ} = \Delta S_{sys} - \frac{\Delta H_{sys}}{T}$ so $-T \Delta S_{univ} =  \Delta H_{sys} - T \Delta S_{sys}$ which relates to Gibbs equation making $\Delta G = \Delta H_{sys} - T \Delta S_{sys}$. When $\Delta G < 0$ a reaction is spontaneous where $\Delta G > 0$ nonspontaneous and $\Delta G = 0$ the reaction is at equilibrium.

Standard Gibbs change in energy $\Delta G°$ is the energy change when all components are in their standard state and can be found $\Delta G° = \Delta H° - T\Delta S°$. Standard Gibbs energy of formation $\Delta G°_f$ is the Gibbs energy change when one mole of a compound is made from its elements in their standard states $\Delta G°_f = \sum m \Delta G°_f (products) - \sum n \Delta G°_f (reactants)$.

$\Delta G$ is the maximum useful work that can be done by a system during a spontaneous process at constant T and P or the minimum work that must be done to a system to make a nonspontaneous process occur at constant T and P. While $\Delta G$ is a maximum of useful work the amount of actual work done is always less because to do the maximum amount of work Gibbs energy would have to be released reversibly for an infinite amount of steps where real processes have a finite amounts of steps and heat is always lost in the process and not entirely transferred. Efficiency is the measure of the percentage of work that is outputted relative to the energy input. $\Delta G_{sys} < 0$ does work on the surroundings (-w) and actual work done is less than the maximum. $\Delta G_{sys} > 0$ work is done on the system (+w) and will be more than the minimum energy since heat is lost in the process. $\Delta G_{sys} = 0$ the process is at equilibrium and can no longer do work.

If $\Delta H < 0$ and $\Delta S > 0$ then $\Delta G < 0$ and the reaction is spontaneous at all temperatures. If $\Delta H > 0$ and $\Delta S < 0$ then $\Delta G > 0$ and the reaction is nonspontaneous at all temperature. If $\Delta H > 0$ and $\Delta S > 0$ then $\Delta G < 0$ at high temperatures and spontaneous but $\Delta G > 0$ at low temperatures and nonspontaneous. If $\Delta H < 0$ and $\Delta S < 0$ then $\Delta G < 0$ at low temperatures and spontaneous but $\Delta G > 0$ at high temperatures and nonspontaneous. For reactions that can be spontaneous or nonspontaneous the temperature value where one becomes the other can be found by setting $\Delta G = 0$ and solving for temperature $\Delta G = \Delta H - T \Delta S = 0$ so $\Delta H = T \Delta S$ and $T = \frac{\Delta H}{\Delta S}$. A nonspontaneous step can be driven by a spontaneous step through the coupling of reactions where a spontaneous step supplies enough Gibbs energy to make a nonspontaneous step occur.

$\Delta G$ is related to the equilibrium constants $Q$ and $K$ through both calculating the direction of a reaction. When $ln(\frac{Q}{K})$ is less than greater than or equal to zero so is $\Delta G$ and $\Delta G = RT ln(\frac{Q}{K}) = RTln(Q) - RTln(K)$. In this equation if $Q$ has standard state values then $\Delta G = \Delta G°$ and since $Q = 1$ then $RTln(Q) = 0$ so $\Delta G° = -RTln(K)$. Due to logarithmic nature a small change in $\Delta G°$ causes a large change in $K$ and as $\Delta G°$ becomes more positive $K$ becomes smaller and as $\Delta G°$ becomes more negative $K$ becomes larger. Since $\Delta G° = -RTln(K)$ the $-RTln(K)$ can be replaced by $\Delta G°$ in $\Delta G = RTln(Q) - RTln(K)$ so $\Delta G = \Delta G° + RTln(Q)$ which can be used for reactions not in standard states. When $K > 1$ and $\Delta G° < 0$ the reaction is product favored or exergonic and when $K < 1$ and $\Delta G° > 0$ the reaction is reactant favored or endergonic.

The initial energy needed to initiate a spontaneous reaction is call the activation energy.
