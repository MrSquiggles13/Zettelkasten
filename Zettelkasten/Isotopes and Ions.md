# Isotopes and Ions

## Notes

### Isotopes

Isotopes are elements that differ in the natural amount of neutrons.
Elements have an atomic number which is the number of protons in an element's nucleus and a mass number which is protons plus neutrons. To find the number of neutrons subtract atomic number from the mass number. 

Atomic mass is the actual measure mass of an element atom and is very close to the mass number. Atomic mass is calculated in Atomic Mass Units (amu) which is $\frac{1}{12}$ the mass of a Carbon-12 atom.
Relative atomic mass is the weighted average mass of all naturally occurring isotopes.

In modern terms the Atomic Mass Unit is known as the Dalton (da).

Mass spectrometry is measures the percentage of an elements different isotopes in a collections by knocking off an electron of the element and sending it through a magnet that separates the sample by charge to mass ratio. This helps calculate the relative atomic weight of elements by a weighted average of each isotope's mass to its natural abundance. Recent studies show that isotopic composition can vary depending on the source of collection and recently mass has been given as an interval for high varying elements yet this is only used in work that involves very high precision.

### Ions

Ions are elements that have more or less electrons than the atoms neutral state.
Cations are when the atom has a positive charge and loses electrons.
Anions are when the atom has a negative charge and gains electrons.

Ionic compounds use formula units instead of atomic mass but is essentially the same except that ionic compound are not physically separated into units.

Ions bond together to form ionic compounds which have a strong attraction to each other because of their opposite polarities which results in a net 0 charge. These bonds must have the proper ratio of cation to anion to have a net zero charge i.e. CaCl<sub>2</sub> -> Ca<sup>2-</sup> + (Cl<sup>-</sup>)<sub>2</sub> These ionic compounds are typically between a metal cation (except for NH<sub>4</sub><sup>+</sup>) and a non-metal anion which form a lattice structure. i.e. NaCl (table salt or also known as the mineral halite).

Ionic compounds dissolve in water where the cation and anion separate. In an aqueous solution they are called electrolytes since they are good conductors of electricity. Ionic compounds are also good conductors in molten (liquid) form. Ionic compounds form hard, brittle, crystalline solids, have a very high boiling and melting point and density.

Metals that are not monatomic ions have variance in their charges which are labelled with roman numerals. Charges of the metal are derived from the anion's charge.

Hydrated ionic compounds have a specific number of water (H<sub>2</sub>O) molecules per formula unit. The formula name for these compounds compos of the ionic compound followed by the Greek prefix representing the amount of water molecules followed by hydrate i.e. MgCl<sub>2</sub> $\cdot$ 3H<sub>2</sub>O is magnesium chloride trihydrate.
