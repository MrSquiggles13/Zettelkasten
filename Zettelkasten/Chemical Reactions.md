# Chemical Reactions

## Notes

There are different classes of chemical reactions such as:
- Decomposition - one reactant that is a compound produces two elements or smaller compounds CD --> C + D
- Combination - two reactants that are elements or compounds produce one compound A + B --> AB
- Single Displacement - two reactants that are an element and a compound produce an element and a compound of different composition A + CD --> C + AD
- Double Displacement - two reactants that are compounds produce two compounds of different composition CD + EF --> CF + ED
- Combustion - an element or compound reacts with oxygen and generates flame. Can sometimes be considered a combination reaction 2Mg<sub>(s)</sub> + O<sub>2</sub><sub>(g)</sub> --> 2MgO<sub>(s)</sub>. Combustion of hydrocarbon (C<sub>x</sub>H<sub>y</sub>) or hydrocarbon with oxygen (C<sub>x</sub>H<sub>y</sub>O<sub>z</sub>) produces carbon dioxide gas (CO<sub>2</sub><sub>(g)</sub>) and water vapor (H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>(g)</sub>)

There are three types of double displacement reactions also called metathesis reactions
1. Precipitation - a solid precipitate forms based on the solubility rules and the other compound remains dissolved in aqueous form. example: NaCl(aq) + AgNO<sub>3</sub>(aq) -> AgCl(s) + NaNO<sub>3</sub>(aq)
2. Acid - Base Neutralization - when an acid and a base mix a new ionic compound is formed and the hydronium in acid form with the hydroxide in the base to make water H<sub>2</sub>O. example: HNO<sub>3</sub>(aq) + NaOH (aq) -> NaNO<sub>3</sub>(aq) + H<sub>2</sub>O(l) 
3. Gas Formation - a gas is formed as a product of the reaction and is typically a metal ionic compound reacting with an acid. example: CaCO<sub>3</sub>(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> CaCl<sub>2</sub>(aq) + H<sub>2</sub>O(l) + CO<sub>2</sub>(g) and in this example it is a double displacement followed by a decomposition since the product is actually H<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub> which breaks down to water and carbon dioxide

Displacement is when a more reactive element or compound replaces a less reactive element or compound in a reaction to form a new substance. Halogens and metals are typically observed displacing each other according to the activity series or displacement rules. Halogen reactivity increases the higher up on the group 17 column of the periodic table.

Combustion analysis is a method commonly used to measure the amounts of carbon and hydrogen in a combustible organic compound.

Side reactions are unexpected or unwanted chemical reactions that consumes some of the reactant and produces a lower yield.
Multi-step synthesis can generate a very low yield even if the yield between step is very high. Each diminish in yield compounds by the next step.

Green chemistry is the field of study in developing methos to reduce or prevent the release of harmful substances into the environment. This involves atom economy or the proportion of reactant atoms that end up in the desired product.

Thermal decomposition is a decomposition reaction that involves high heat.

Oxidation-Reduction reactions are when electrons are transferred between two elements, molecules or ions. Oxidation number (or state) is the hypothetical charge an atom would have if its bond to other elements were ionic. Oxidation is when there is a loss of electrons and oxidation number goes up while reduction is a gain of electrons and oxidation number goes down. Example: Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> MgCl<sub>2</sub>(aq) + H<sub>2</sub>(g) Mg lost electrons so was oxidized (0 -> 2) and H gained electrons so was reduced (1 -> 0). 

Single displacement reactions are always redox reactions as well as combustion reactions.

 Typically the farther top right of the periodic table (more electronegative) the element is the more likely the element will have the same oxidation number as its ionic form. The activity series also show the ease of oxidation with the bottom of the table the least ease of oxidation and the top the most.

Solution stoichiometry helps to measure the amounts of reactants and products involved in a reaction such as precipitate formed or acid neutralized by measuring the amount of moles in a reactant or product and using that information to derive the amount of moles of another substance using molarity and volume to derived moles and mass.

Titration is the process of incrementally adding solution A to solution B where A's molarity is known and B's is not with an indicator chemical that reacts only when a certain amount of A-B reaction has occurred. For example is determining an acid by adding a base and an indicator solution that changes color when the entire solution added to is basic or acidic.

Reversable reactions are chemical reactions where the reactants produce products that can react to produce the original reactants. This process continues until the reaction is the same rate forward as it is backward which is the equilibrium. example: Calcium carbonate that is heated in a closed container CaCO<sub>3</sub>(s) <--> CaO(s) + CO<sub>2</sub>(g) note: if gas escaped reaction would not reverse.

### Equilibrium

Chemical equilibrium is a state reached by a chemical reaction where there is no change in the concentrations of reactants or products which means the forward and reverse reactions are occurring at the same rate. This equalization of the relative concentrations is represented by the equilibrium constant $K_{eq}$ which is a ratio of the concentrations of reactants and products at a given temperature. Given a chemical equation $aA + bB \leftrightarrow cC + dD$ then $K_{eq} = \frac{[C]^c \cdot [D]^d}{[A]^a \cdot [B]^b}$ where brackets around a chemical is representative of the concentration.

In principle all reactions are reversible and reach equilibrium. Some are considered negligible so either there is no significant reaction or the reactants turn almost entirely into products so is considered just a forward reaction.

The value of $K_{eq}$ determines equilibrium favorability or whether the product side is favored ($K_{eq} \gt 1$) the reactant side is favored ($K_{eq} \lt 1$) or reactants and products are favored similarly ($K_{eq} \approx 1$).

Heterogenous equilibrium reactions are reactions that involve chemicals in more than one type of state. In these reactions solids and liquids are left out of the equilibrium equation as they do not change concentration. $aA(aq) + bB(l) \leftrightarrow cC(g) + dD(s)$ B and D are left our $K_{eq} = \frac{[C]^c(g)}{[A]^a(aq)}$.

The reaction quotient denoted $Q$ helps determine which direction a reaction will favor to reach equilibrium and is written similarly to K except the concentrations are the current concentrations of the reaction $Q = \frac{[C]^c \cdot [D]^d}{[A]^a \cdot [B]^b}$. Since $K_c$ cannot be used if the reaction is not in equilibrium the comparison of Q and K will determine which side of the equation is favored. When $Q \lt K$ the products or right side is favored $Q \gt K$ the reactants or left side is favored and $Q = K$ the reaction is at equilibrium. K determines how far toward the products does the position of equilibrium lie while Q determines which direction the reaction will proceed. K does not indicate the rate of reaction just the equilibrium amount. 

The Law of Chemical Equilibrium or Law of Mass Action states that when a system reaches equilibrium that at a given temperature the ratio of quantities that make up the reaction quotient has a constant numerical value. This means a reactions will always adjust the reactants and products concentrations to achieve K no matter the initial values.

The Q or K of the forward reaction is the reciprocal of the reverse reaction i.e. $Q_f = \frac{1}{Q_r}$. Equations multiplied by a common factor $n(aA + bB \leftrightarrow cC + dD)$ adjust the Q and K values by $Q^n \ or \ K^n = (\frac{[C]^c \cdot [D]^d}{[A]^a \cdot [B]^b})^n$. If a reaction is the sum of two or more reactions then $Q = Q_1 \cdot Q_1 \cdot Q_2 ... Q_n$.

Reactions involving gases can use partial pressures instead of concentrations $K_p = \frac{P_C^c \cdot P_D^d}{P_A^a \cdot P_B^b}$. $K_c$ relates to $K_p$ through the ideal gas law which can be rearranged in terms of concentration $\frac{P}{RT} = \frac{n}{V} = concentration$ which can be equated to $K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n_{gas}}$ where $\Delta n_{gas} = n_{products} - n_{reactants}$.

Le Chatelier's principle states that an equilibrium reaction will respond to a stress by reacting in either direction of the equation to reestablish equilibrium defined by K.

Increasing the amount of a product in a system will decrease the other products to react with the introduced product amount and increase the amount of reactants and vice versa while increasing the amount of a reactant in a system will decrease the other reactants and increase the amount of products and vice versa.

Temperature change affects the value of K itself which can be determined by first concluding if a reactions is either exothermic or endothermic. Heat can be represented as either a product if exothermic or a reactant if endothermic. If temperature is increased on an endothermic reaction then the heat increases amount of reactants causing K to increase and vice vera while on an exothermic reaction the heat increases amount of products causing K to decrease and vice versa.

Change in volume and pressure causes a change in concentration of gases affecting equilibrium. When volume decreases the reaction will favor the side with fewer moles of gas to compensate for the pressure increase while when volume increases the reaction will favor the side with more moles to compensate for the pressure decrease. This can be shown by comparing the new gas concentrations in the reaction quotient Q to the equilibrium constant K and how the concentrations will increase or decrease affecting the ratio and being comparatively lower or higher than K.  

Catalysts do not affect the position of equilibrium only the rate at which a reaction reaches equilibrium.
